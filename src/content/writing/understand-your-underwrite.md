When an owner is turned down for a business loan, the reason given is usually short and unsatisfying. After years on the lending side of the desk, I can tell you the real decision was made across a handful of questions the underwriter asked about your file. If you know those questions, you can answer them before you apply.

## The underwriter's job

An underwriter isn't trying to find a reason to say no. Their job is to document that the loan will be repaid — and that if it isn't, the bank has a second way out. Everything in your file is read through that lens. Most lenders organize it around the familiar "Cs of credit":

- **Capacity** — can the business's cash flow repay the debt?
- **Capital** — how much of your own money is in the deal?
- **Collateral** — what secures the loan if cash flow fails?
- **Character** — what does your credit history and track record say about how you handle obligations?
- **Conditions** — what are the loan's purpose, the industry and the economy doing?

Capacity comes first. A strong file on the other four rarely rescues a weak one on cash flow.

## Capacity is measured with debt service coverage

The core number is the **debt service coverage ratio (DSCR)**: the cash flow available to pay debt, divided by the total debt payments due over the same period.

A DSCR of 1.00x means every dollar of cash flow goes to debt payments — no cushion at all. Most commercial lenders want to see a meaningful margin above that, commonly somewhere around 1.20x to 1.25x, though requirements vary by lender, loan type and industry.

Two details trip owners up:

1. **Lenders use your tax returns, not your internal books.** If you've minimized taxable income aggressively, you've also minimized the cash flow a lender can count. Add-backs such as depreciation, interest and certain one-time expenses help — but only if you can document them.
2. **Lenders look at the *global* picture.** For most small businesses, the owners personally guarantee the loan, so the underwriter combines business cash flow with the owners' personal income and personal debts. A strong business can fail a global DSCR test because of a large personal mortgage or car payments.

I built a global debt service coverage worksheet for exactly this reason: so owners and non-banker advisors can see the number the lender will calculate *before* the application goes in.

## Commercial real estate: owner-occupied vs. investment

Real estate loans are underwritten differently depending on who occupies the building.

- **Owner-occupied** property is underwritten mostly on the operating business's cash flow. Programs like the **SBA 504** can reduce the down payment required for owner-occupied buildings and equipment.
- **Non-owner-occupied (investment)** property is underwritten on the property's own rental income. Leases, occupancy history and market rents matter more than the owner's business.

Construction and renovation loans add another layer: a draw schedule, a budget with contingency, and often a plan to convert to permanent financing — sometimes through a shorter-term **mini-perm** loan — once the project is complete and stabilized.

## What to fix before you apply

1. **Run your own DSCR** — business and global — using your last two to three years of tax returns.
2. **Know your down payment.** Have the equity injection in hand and be able to show where it came from.
3. **Write a use-of-funds statement** that ties every dollar to a purpose.
4. **Prepare projections** that connect to your historical numbers. Lenders are skeptical of a hockey stick with no explanation.
5. **Clean up your personal credit** and be ready to explain anything unusual.
6. **Match the loan to the lender.** Banks, credit unions, SBA lenders and CDFIs each have different appetites. A "no" from one is often a "yes" from another.

A lender who understands your file is a lender who can advocate for it in credit committee. The more of this work you do up front, the easier you make their job — and the better your terms tend to be.
