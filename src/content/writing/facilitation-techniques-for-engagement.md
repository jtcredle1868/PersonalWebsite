Most of us who teach adults were taught by lecture, so we teach by lecture. It works — up to a point. The research and my own experience in accounting classrooms, SBDC workshops and conference breakouts say the same thing: people remember what they *work out* far better than what they are *told*.

Facilitated learning shifts the instructor from the sole source of answers to the guide of a discussion. You still bring the expertise; you just let the room do more of the thinking. Here are the four skills that make the shift possible.

## 1. Listening is key

There are three levels of listening, and only one of them facilitates learning:

- **Marginal listening** — you hear the words while you plan your next point. Participants can tell.
- **Evaluative listening** — you judge each comment as right or wrong as it arrives. It shuts down anyone who isn't sure.
- **Active listening** — you listen for the idea underneath the words, reflect it back, and build on it.

Active listening is what makes a participant willing to speak a second time.

## 2. Observing is critical: look, think, act

Watch the room the way a pilot scans instruments. **Look** for crossed arms, blank stares, side conversations, someone who has started to speak twice and stopped. **Think** about what it means — confusion, disagreement, boredom, fear of looking foolish. Then **act**: slow down, ask a question, change the activity, or invite the quiet person in by name.

## 3. Attending must occur

Attending is how you physically signal that the room has your attention.

| Do | Don't |
| --- | --- |
| Remove barriers between you and the participants | Distance yourself physically |
| Position yourself so you can see everyone | Focus only on the people directly in front of you |
| Use visuals as enhancements | Run a slide-driven lecture and talk to the screen |
| Scan the room and keep eye contact | Turn your back to part of the group |
| Move around the room | Stay in one fixed spot — or race around it |
| Smile and nod affirmatively | Answer a question you asked as a discussion starter |
| Give people time to answer | Respond to every statement with a one-word "Right!" |

The hardest one on the list is silence. Count to seven after you ask a question. Someone will fill it.

## 4. Facilitation is questioning

Three kinds of questions do most of the work:

- **Overhead questions** go to the whole group: "What makes a lender nervous about a startup?" They open the discussion without putting anyone on the spot.
- **Direct questions** go to one person by name. Use them to draw in someone with relevant experience — or someone who has gone quiet.
- **Relay and redirect** questions hand a participant's question back to the group: "Good question — who has run into that?" This is the move that turns a Q&A into a discussion, and it tells the room their experience counts.

## Tools that help

- **Facilitated storytelling.** Ask participants to share a real situation, then use it as the case study for the concept.
- **Brainstorming.** Warm up with a quick game, break into small groups when energy slows, and capture every idea — on a whiteboard or a laptop projected for the room.
- **Online facilitation.** Mobile quiz apps, word mosaics and digital whiteboards give remote and hybrid participants a way in.

## Remember who is in the room

Adult learners bring worries a traditional student may not: fear of looking foolish, of being the oldest in the group, of new technology, of assessment. They're juggling family and work. They sometimes doubt whether their experience is worth sharing. Facilitation answers those worries directly — it makes their experience the curriculum.

## Next steps

End every facilitated session with action: what should be researched further, what needs to go back to a larger group, and which ideas turn into action items. A good discussion that doesn't lead anywhere is still just a nice conversation.
