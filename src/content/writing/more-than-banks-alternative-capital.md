A declined bank loan isn't the end of a financing plan. More often it's a sign the request is in the wrong place. Traditional banks and SBA-guaranteed loans are only part of the capital market available to small businesses, and some of the best-fit money for a young or unconventional company lives elsewhere.

Here are the alternatives I walk clients through, roughly in the order I consider them.

## Credit unions

Many credit unions run member business lending programs, and their credit appetite can differ meaningfully from a bank's. Relationship and community ties often carry more weight. If you're already a member, start there.

## Community Development Financial Institutions (CDFIs)

CDFIs are mission-driven lenders certified to serve markets and borrowers that conventional lenders underserve. They often accept thinner collateral, shorter operating histories or lower credit scores — and many pair the loan with technical assistance. Rates may be higher than a bank's but far lower than most online alternatives.

## Regional development authorities and public programs

Local and regional development authorities, revolving loan funds and state programs can provide gap financing — the piece that sits between the bank's loan and the owner's equity. They are frequently tied to job creation or location, so be ready to document your impact.

## Fintech and online lenders

Online lenders are fast and use their own data-driven underwriting. That speed has a price: effective rates and fees can be high and repayment terms short. They can make sense for a clearly defined, short-term, high-return need. Read every term, and calculate the true annual cost before you sign.

## Peer-to-peer lending

Peer-to-peer platforms connect borrowers directly with individual lenders. They can fill a gap for smaller amounts, but terms vary widely and personal credit usually drives pricing.

## Crowdfunding

- **Rewards crowdfunding** pre-sells a product to your future customers. It raises money *and* validates demand.
- **Investment crowdfunding** lets you raise capital — debt or equity — from many smaller investors under federal crowdfunding rules. It takes more preparation and disclosure, but it can turn loyal customers into owners.

## Private equity and angel investment

Equity isn't a loan. You're selling part of the company, and investors expect growth large enough to return their money several times over. That makes equity right for businesses built to scale quickly — and wrong for many excellent businesses that are designed to be steady and profitable.

If equity does fit, preparation matters: a disciplined pitch deck, a clear use of funds, projections investors can believe, and a realistic sense of which stage of investor you're approaching. Local angel networks and pitch competitions are good first steps.

## Build a capital stack, not a single loan

The strongest financing plans usually combine sources: owner equity, a senior bank or credit union loan, gap financing from a CDFI or development authority, perhaps grant or crowdfunding dollars on top. Each layer reduces the risk the others take on. I've seen clients assemble debt, equity, grant and crowdfunded capital into a single project that no one source would have financed alone.

The question to ask isn't "Will the bank lend me this?" It's "What combination of capital fits this business, at this stage, for this purpose?"
